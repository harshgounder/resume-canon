# 18. Narrative and credibility

How a reader reconstructs a career from fragments, and what they can verify.

> Evidence: wave 08 lane N (narrative, credibility, interview linkage, 9,452
> words, 94 sources).
> [research/wave-08/narrative-credibility-interview.md](../research/wave-08/narrative-credibility-interview.md)

## The reader builds a story whether you write one or not

Nobody reads a resume as a list. They reconstruct a narrative: where this person
came from, why they moved, what they are good at, why this role. The fragments
they use are titles, employers, dates, and the first line of each bullet.

That reconstruction is where positioning happens. It is not a summary section. It
is the coherence of the fragments.

Two tests for coherence:

1. Read only the titles and dates. Does the sequence make sense without any
   bullets?
2. Read only the first line of each bullet. Does that tell a consistent story?

If either fails, the document is asking the reader to do work they will not do.

## Gaps

The instinct is to explain. The evidence says explanation does not pay.

In a UK field audit of 9,022 applications, resumes with an explained gap and
resumes with an unexplained gap scored the same: b = -0.050 against -0.049,
d = -0.10. The explanation moved nothing.

What did move callbacks was a format change: years worked instead of exact dates,
gaining 8 percent against no-gap resumes and 15 percent against gap resumes (V2).

So the lever is the date format, not the sentence. And never switch to a
functional resume: it removes the dated records that recruiter filters read, so
you lose the search match as well as the explanation.

## The founder penalty

Founder experience is not neutral on a resume. In one study, male ex-founders had
a 35 percent lower interview likelihood than comparable employees, 13 percent
callback against 7 percent (Kacperczyk and Younkin, Organization Science, 2022).
A second audit put founder resumes 23 to 29 percent less likely to be a top
choice. There was no comparable penalty for women in the first study.

The mechanism is a suspicion: why would a founder want this job, and how long
will they stay. The fix is not to hide the founder role, which creates a gap. The
fix is to answer the suspicion directly: the function you actually performed, the
scale, and why this role is the next step.

## Credibility is what a reader can check in a minute

| Signal | Verification cost | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Employer brand | Zero, known | High, but says little about you |
| Title and dates | One call, or a screening service | High, and easy to contradict |
| Named project with a link | One click | High |
| Open source contribution to someone else's project | Two minutes, and costly to fake | High |
| Personal repo with stars | One click, weak | Medium |
| Certificates and badges | One lookup | Low, 3.8 percent first relevant credential |
| Self-assessment | None, unverifiable | Zero |

**GitHub needs a caveat.** Stars and commit counts are cheap signals. A live
check in the wave found that a major project with 54,732 stars had 7 of its last
8 commits from a dependency bot. Commit counts deflate in two minutes of
inspection, and employers discount forks and watchers. What holds up is accepted
contributions to projects you do not own, and a repository with tests, a README,
and a history that looks like work.

**Credentials pay only when relevant.** Brookings, across 37.7 million resumes,
found a 3.8 percent first-call gain for a job-relevant credential, about 1
percent for each additional one, and 1.8 percent for an irrelevant one. Badges
are 0.41 percent of 1.5 million unique credentials. A certificate is a small
nudge, not a differentiator.

## Screening finds discrepancies, and titles are the fault line

In HireRight's 2025 survey, more than three quarters of respondents found
discrepancies in the previous 12 months, and employment verification was the top
area (64 percent EMEA, 72 percent APAC). The report places date and title
mismatches and fabricated employment on one spectrum.

Practical consequence: a title you inflated is not a small embellishment, it is
the exact field a verification service checks. The safe move is the honest
internal title plus the posted title in parentheses, which is also what the
parsing chapter recommends for a different reason.

## Interview linkage

Which claims get probed is set by the interview's structure more than by your
resume.

- Structured interviews have a validity of about 0.42, unstructured about 0.19
  (Sackett, 2022). The structured ones probe the same dimensions for everyone.
- Probing improves the completeness of a story, and untrained interviewers accept
  "pseudostories", abstract generic accounts that sound like answers.

So the interview does not punish specificity, it punishes abstraction. A bullet
that says "improved performance" invites a generic question and a generic answer.
A bullet that says "cut p99 from 900ms to 210ms by replacing the N+1 query"
invites a specific one, which you can answer.

## What to do

| Situation | Move |
|---|---|
| Fragmented career | Make the titles and dates tell a coherent sequence |
| Gap | Date it honestly, consider years-worked format, skip the explanatory line |
| Founder or self-employed | Name the function, the scale, and why this role |
| Weak verification | Add one checkable artifact, not five claims |
| GitHub | One repo with tests and history beats ten forks |
| Certificates | Keep only job-relevant ones, they are a nudge |
| Interview prep | For every bullet, be ready for the question it invites |
